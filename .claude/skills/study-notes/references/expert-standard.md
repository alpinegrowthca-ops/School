# The Professor-Level Standard

Notes from this skill should read like the best course notes written at top universities: the kind a professor would hand out, or that a top student who has mastered the course would write. They are not a student's summary of slides. This file defines that standard and the review pass that enforces it.

The Concept Ladder (`teaching-concepts.md`) makes notes *teach*. This standard makes them *expert*: organized the way the discipline thinks, precise, evidenced, and aimed at what examiners actually reward.

---

## 1. The eight hallmarks of professor-level notes

### H1. Organized around big ideas, not around slides
Experts' knowledge is organized around a field's **core principles**, and they file details under them. Novices organize around surface features (Chi, Feltovich & Glaser, 1981; Bransford, Brown & Cocking, *How People Learn*, 2000).
- Open every note with **Core principles**: the 1–3 deep ideas this lecture is really about, stated as claims ("Reaction rate is limited by how much enzyme is occupied"), not topics ("Enzyme kinetics").
- Each section says which principle it serves. Details hang off principles.
- The course hub keeps a running list of the course's **Big ideas**, and each note links to them.

### H2. Conditionalized knowledge: when, not just what
Experts know *when* knowledge applies: the cues that signal it, its assumptions, and where it fails (Bransford et al., 2000).
- Every core concept or method gets **Recognition cues** ("you're looking at a competitive inhibitor when…") and **Boundaries** (assumptions, edge cases, regimes where it breaks).
- Problem-solving content gets "When you see ___, think ___" patterns organized by *principle*, not by surface story.

### H3. Rigor and precision
- **Exact definitions** in the course's terminology, with every term in a definition already defined.
- **Explicit assumptions and conditions** for every formula, law and model.
- **Consistent notation** across the course: record symbols in the hub's *Notation & conventions* table, and flag it when a slide or textbook uses different notation.
- **Units and quantifiers** always ("for all", "at least one", "approximately", "in the limit"). No vague verbs like "affects", "is related to" or "impacts": say *how* (increases, causes, is necessary for, correlates with).
- **Epistemic status labels** on key claims, so the student knows what *kind* of statement it is:

| Label | Means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| `[Def]` | Definition or convention, true by agreement | Km is the [S] giving ½Vmax |
| `[Law]` / `[Thm]` | Established law or proven theorem (state its conditions) | Michaelis–Menten under steady-state assumptions |
| `[Finding]` | Empirical result: who found it and how robust it is | Hexokinase Km ≈ 0.1 mM |
| `[Model]` | Simplification useful within limits | The steady-state model |
| `[Interp]` | Interpretation, theory or position that is debated | Competing historical interpretations |

Use the labels on the claims that matter, not on every bullet.

### H4. "How do we know?": the evidence
Professors care about *why we believe* a claim, and science and social science exams often ask about it.
- For each major claim, a short **How do we know?** block: the key experiment, data, argument or primary source; what it showed; and its main limitation.
- Name researchers and dates *only when the sources give them or you are certain*. Otherwise describe the evidence generically and add `[VERIFY]`. **Never invent a study, author, date, statistic or quotation.**

### H5. Derivations, proofs and arguments, not just results
- Show where results come from: a full derivation, or a sketch of the key steps with the *idea* behind each step.
- For mathematical courses, use the textbook structure of elite course notes: numbered **Definition 5.1**, **Theorem 5.2** (with hypotheses), **Proof** (or proof sketch, labelled as such), **Example**, **Remark** (subtleties, common traps, extensions).
- For argument-based courses, give the *structure* of the argument: premises → inference → conclusion, and where critics attack it.

### H6. Context and significance
Brief and labelled `[Added]` when it isn't from the lecture:
- **Origin**: what problem prompted the idea, and what it replaced.
- **Significance**: why it matters in the field, and what it makes possible.
- **Frontier**: limitations, open questions and current debates, at the course's level.

### H7. Synthesis
- Connections to earlier and later lectures, the readings, and the course's Big ideas.
- Contrast with competing models, theories or methods: what each explains that the others don't.
- At Deep depth, one **Through-line** sentence: how this lecture advances the course's overall story.

### H8. The examiner's view
- **Professor's-eye view** section: the 3–5 questions an examiner would most likely ask about this lecture, based on learning objectives, exam signals and past exams. For each, what a **full-credit answer** must contain, and the **typical B-level answer** that loses marks (what it omits or gets imprecise).
- Distinguish what must be *memorized* (definitions, key values) from what must be *understood and applied*.

## 1b. How the core research articles shape this standard

The student supplied these eight sources as the foundation for the skill. Each hallmark above is built to satisfy them:

| Source | Finding | Where it shows up in a professor-level note |
|---|---|---|
| **Dunlosky et al. (2013)**, *Improving Students' Learning* | Practice testing and spacing are high-utility. Elaboration and self-explanation are moderate, and need prior knowledge. Highlighting, summarizing and rereading are low-utility | Check-yourself and Professor's-eye-view questions (testing); the "Before you start" box (prior knowledge before elaboration); derivations and "why" chains (self-explanation). Bold is never used as highlighting, and summaries are synthesis, not the study method |
| **Roediger & Karpicke (2006)**, *Test-Enhanced Learning* | Repeated retrieval beats restudying at 1 week (61% vs 40%), but rereaders feel more confident | Notes end in retrieval, not in a re-readable recap. The Professor's-eye view turns the note into an exam to answer, not a page to reread. The first quiz is the next day |
| **The Learning Scientists**, *Six Strategies* | Elaboration: ask how and why, and compare similar ideas. Concrete examples: explain *how* each one illustrates the idea. Dual coding: pair words with visuals. Interleaving: mix, but don't switch too often | H2 recognition cues and matrices (similarities and differences); "Shows it because" lines on every example (Concept Ladder rung 6); diagrams beside the explaining text (rung 9); interleaved practice exams |
| **U. Michigan CRLT No. 16** (DeZure, Kaplan & Deerman, 2001) | Reviewing complete, well-organized notes, even someone else's, aids recall. Instructor cues and organizing frameworks help | Completeness through the coverage audit; the Core principles + Roadmap as the organizing framework; exam signals captured as the instructor's cues |
| **Luo, Kiewra & Samuelson (2016)**, *Revising Lecture Notes* | Revising (adding and connecting) beats recopying, especially on **relationship** questions. Revising during pauses beats revising after | H1 and H7 make relationships explicit (principles → details, cross-lecture links, competing models); "What your notes missed" as a revision task; pause-and-revise advice for recordings |
| **Cornell University LSC**, *The Cornell Note-taking System* | Record → Questions → Recite → Reflect → Review, plus a Summary | Questions = Check yourself + Professor's-eye view; Recite = answer aloud before opening; Reflect = Synthesis and Through-line (H7); Summary = a prose synthesis answering the Big question |
| **University of New England**, *Cornell Note Taking System* | The 5 Rs, with **Reduce**: condense notes to key words and questions soon after class | Core principles and recognition cues *are* the reduction: the lecture distilled to the ideas and cues an expert would keep |
| **Wilfrid Laurier University**, *Cornell Note Taking Strategies* | Cue-column questions, recitation, and regular spaced review rather than rereading | Weekly 10-minute recite-not-reread review in Next steps; cue questions written to test relationships, not just facts |

## 2. Discipline-specific expert features

| Discipline | What a professor-level note adds |
|---|---|
| Math, theoretical CS | Definition/Theorem/Proof/Remark structure; hypotheses checked in examples; counterexamples showing why each hypothesis is needed |
| Physics, engineering | Physical interpretation of every term; dimensional analysis; limiting cases; order-of-magnitude estimates; assumptions (ideal, frictionless, linear regime) |
| Chemistry, biochemistry | Mechanisms with electron flow and *why* (thermodynamic vs kinetic control); structure → function; quantitative values with units |
| Biology, medicine | Mechanism → physiology → clinical or real consequence; the key experiments; regulation and what happens when it fails |
| Economics, social sciences | Model assumptions; predictions; the empirical evidence for and against; identification problems (correlation vs causation) |
| History | Causation (long-term, short-term, contingency); historiography (how interpretations differ and why); primary-source evidence |
| Philosophy, theory | Argument reconstruction; key objections and replies; precise distinctions between positions |
| Literature | Thesis-driven readings; textual evidence with close reading; critical lenses; context |
| Law | Rule → elements → exceptions → policy rationale; how courts have applied it; open questions |

## 3. Anti-patterns (what a professor would circle in red)

- **Slide transcription**: bullets copied from slides with no explanation, relationships or reasoning.
- **Vague verbs and hedges**: "X affects Y", "is important for", "plays a role in". Say *how* and *how much*.
- **Undefined jargon**, or a term used before its definition.
- **Unqualified absolutes** ("always", "never") where the truth has conditions.
- **Results without reasons**: a formula with no derivation, conditions or meaning.
- **False precision or invented specifics**: made-up numbers, citations, dates or quotations. These are the worst error, because they destroy trust in everything else.
- **Laundry lists**: ten facts with no organizing principle.
- **Padding**: restating the same idea, motivational filler, generic study advice inside content notes.
- **Flattening disagreement**: presenting a debated interpretation as settled fact.

## 4. The Professor Review (mandatory before saving a note)

After drafting, and after the coverage and accuracy audits, re-read the whole note **as a demanding professor in this field would**. Score each criterion from 1 to 5. Anything below 4 must be revised before saving. Then re-score.

| # | Criterion | 5 means |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Accuracy** | Nothing false. Numbers recomputed. `[Added]` claims certain or flagged `[VERIFY]` |
| 2 | **Precision** | Exact definitions, explicit assumptions, consistent notation, units, no vague verbs |
| 3 | **Completeness** | Coverage audit passed. Every exam signal is present |
| 4 | **Depth** | Mechanisms, derivations or arguments and evidence ("How do we know?") for every core claim |
| 5 | **Expert organization** | Core principles up front. Details clearly hang off them |
| 6 | **Conditionalized knowledge** | Recognition cues and boundaries for every core concept or method |
| 7 | **Teaching clarity** | Concept Ladder complete. A student who missed the lecture could learn from it |
| 8 | **Synthesis** | Real connections across lectures, readings and competing ideas |
| 9 | **Exam alignment** | The Professor's-eye view matches objectives and signals. Practice reaches Apply and Analyze levels |
| 10 | **Source fidelity** | Claims are tagged. Course framing and notation respected. Conflicts surfaced, not smoothed over |

Ask these red-pen questions while reviewing:
- "Where would a professor write *'be more precise'*?"
- "Which claim would a professor ask *'how do we know?'* about?"
- "Which step would a strong student ask *'why?'* about?"
- "Is anything here I'm not sure is true?" If so, verify it or flag it.

Report the final scores in one line when you hand the note over (for example, `Professor review: 5/5/5/4/5/5/5/4/5/5`) and name any criterion that is limited by the sources (for example, "Depth 4: the lecture gave no evidence for claim X; flagged for office hours").
