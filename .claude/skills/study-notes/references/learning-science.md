# Learning Science Behind This Skill

Every rule in `SKILL.md` traces back to one of the findings below. When you have to make a judgment call the skill doesn't cover, reason from these principles.

## Evidence tiers at a glance

Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013), *Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques*, Psychological Science in the Public Interest, rated 10 common techniques:

| Utility | Technique | What this skill does with it |
|---|---|---|
| **High** | Practice testing (retrieval practice) | Every note ends in recall questions; quizzes after every lecture; full practice exams |
| **High** | Distributed (spaced) practice | Study plans spread reviews out; flashcards go into a spaced-repetition app |
| Moderate | Interleaved practice | Practice exams and cumulative quizzes mix topics and problem types |
| Moderate | Elaborative interrogation ("why/how is this true?") | "Why" questions in every note; the *Connections* section |
| Moderate | Self-explanation | Worked examples annotated step by step; "explain this step" prompts |
| Low | Summarization | Used only as a bottom-of-note synthesis, never as the main study activity |
| Low | Highlighting / underlining | Never used as a study method; bold is reserved for exam-critical terms |
| Low | Rereading | Discouraged. Notes are built to be *tested from*, not re-read |
| Low | Keyword mnemonic, imagery for text | Used sparingly, only for arbitrary lists that must be memorized |

Dunlosky et al. judged each technique on how well it **generalizes** across four dimensions: *learning conditions* (dosage, timing), *student characteristics* (age, ability, prior knowledge), *materials* (vocabulary, texts, math, science) and *criterion tasks* (recall, comprehension, problem solving, delayed tests). The two high-utility techniques held up across all four. Their practical caveats:

- **Practice testing** works best as **low-stakes**, frequent tests that require **recall rather than recognition**, with **several retrievals of each item spread out over time**, and with **feedback** after the attempt. Practice tests help even when their format differs from the final exam's.
- **Distributed practice**: the review gap should grow with how far away the test is. Their rule of thumb is a lag of about **10–20% of the retention interval** (a test in 10 days: reviews about 1–2 days apart; a test in 6 months: about 3–4 weeks apart). Spacing works for vocabulary, definitions, math and even motor skills.
- **Elaborative interrogation and self-explanation** work best when the student **already has some prior knowledge**. With no basics, "why?" questions produce guesses. Notes must teach the basics first, then prompt elaboration.
- **Interleaving** has its strongest evidence in **math and category learning** (telling problem types or categories apart). Use it for problem practice and confusable concepts, not as a reason to shuffle everything.
- **Summarization** helps only students who have been **trained** to summarize well. It is a useful synthesis step (the Cornell summary), not a main study method.
- **Highlighting** can even hurt inference-level learning, because it isolates fragments instead of connecting them.
- **Rereading** is better spaced than massed, but it still loses head-to-head to practice testing. Use testing in its place.

## 1. Retrieval practice (the testing effect)

- **Roediger & Karpicke (2006), *Test-Enhanced Learning***. Students learned short prose passages.
  - *Experiment 1* (study + restudy vs. study + test): restudying won on a test **5 minutes** later (about 81% vs 75%), but testing won after **2 days** (about 68% vs 54%) and after **1 week** (about 56% vs 42%).
  - *Experiment 2*: SSSS (four study periods) vs SSST (three study, one test) vs STTT (one study, three tests). **After 5 minutes** the order was SSSS 83% > SSST 78% > STTT 71%. **After 1 week** it fully reversed: STTT 61% > SSST 56% > SSSS 40%.
  - The SSSS group **predicted they would remember the most**, and they actually remembered the least.
  - The practice tests gave **no feedback**, so retrieval itself strengthens memory. Feedback adds more on top of that.
  - **Lessons:** (1) immediate performance right after studying is a misleading signal, so test yourself the *next day*; (2) the feeling of knowing that rereading produces is an illusion; (3) repeated retrieval beats repeated reading, even though it feels worse.
- **Karpicke & Blunt (2011, *Science*)**: retrieval practice produced about 50% better long-term retention than building concept maps. The advantage held on inference and comprehension questions, not just facts.
- **Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017)**, a meta-analysis of 272 effect sizes: practice tests beat restudying (g ≈ 0.5–0.6). Effects are **stronger when feedback is given, when practice format matches the final exam format, and when the format is demanding (short answer / free recall > recognition)**.
- **Pretesting / errorful generation (Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009; Richland, Kornell & Kao 2009)**: attempting questions *before* learning improves later learning, even when the first guesses are wrong.

**Implications:** notes are question-driven. The first real quiz on a lecture is scheduled for the *next day*, not straight after reading the note. Every quiz has an answer key with *explanations*. Question formats mirror the real exam. Free-recall and short-answer questions come before multiple choice. A "pre-lecture warm-up" quiz is offered when slides are available before class.

## 2. Spacing (distributed practice)

- **Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler (2008)**, with more than 1,350 learners: the best gap between study sessions depends on how far away the test is. It is roughly **20–40% of the retention interval for a test 1 week away**, falling to **5–10% for a test a year away**.
- Practical translation for a semester exam about 3–6 weeks away: review each lecture about 1 day after, about 1 week after, then about every 1–2 weeks until the exam. Cramming the night before produces good short-term scores and fast forgetting.

**Implications:** `study-plan` mode builds expanding-interval schedules. Flashcards are exported for Anki or another spaced-repetition app.

## 3. Interleaving

- **Rohrer & Taylor (2007)**: on a delayed test, students who practiced math problems interleaved scored about 63%, versus about 20% for students who practiced in blocks, even though blocked practice *felt* easier.
- Interleaving teaches **discrimination**: recognizing *which* method or concept applies, which is exactly what an exam demands and what blocked chapter-end problems never train.

**Implications:** cumulative quizzes and practice exams shuffle topics. Include "which approach applies here?" questions. Pair confusable concepts in compare/contrast items.

## 4. Elaboration, self-explanation and concrete examples

- Asking *why* and *how*, and explaining each step in your own words, builds connected understanding (Dunlosky et al., 2013; Chi's self-explanation research).
- Abstract ideas stick when tied to **multiple, varied concrete examples**, and the student must extract what the examples have in common.

**Implications:** every key concept in notes gets a definition, a plain-language explanation, at least one concrete example, and a "why it matters / how it connects" line.

## 5. Worked examples and cognitive load (Sweller)

- Novices learn more from studying **worked examples** than from solving equivalent problems unaided (the *worked-example effect*). As skill grows, **fade** the examples: remove steps progressively until the learner solves problems independently (the expertise-reversal effect).

**Implications:** for quantitative courses, notes include fully worked examples with the *reasoning* for each step. Practice sets go worked example → partially completed → independent problem.

## 6. Dual coding and organization

- Pairing words with visuals (diagrams, flowcharts, timelines, tables) creates two retrieval routes (Paivio; Mayer's multimedia principles).
- **Kiewra's SOAR** (Select, Organize, Associate, Regulate): students who organized notes into **comparative matrices** and built associations, rather than linear lists, scored substantially higher on tests.

**Implications:** use comparison tables (matrix notes) wherever three or more items share attributes. Include Mermaid diagrams or ASCII flowcharts for processes. Describe slide diagrams in words *and* recreate them.

## 7. Note-taking research

**Two functions of notes (CRLT Occasional Paper No. 16; DeZure, Kaplan & Deerman, University of Michigan, 2001, summarizing Kiewra and others):**
- **Encoding**: the act of taking notes helps learning, because selecting, paraphrasing and organizing is processing.
- **External storage**: *reviewing* notes later helps even more, and the review step is essential. Students recall best from their own notes, but reviewing notes **written by someone else (another student or the instructor) works almost as well**. Complete, well-organized notes help most.
- What helps note-takers: pauses, explicit cues about what's important, organizing frameworks (outlines, skeletal or partial notes, matrices) and training in note-taking.

**How complete are student notes? (Kiewra):** students typically record only about **one third** of a lecture's important ideas, and note completeness correlates with achievement.

**Revising notes (Luo, Kiewra & Samuelson, 2016, *Instructional Science*):**
- **Revision effect**: students who *revised* their notes (adding, fixing and connecting ideas) recorded more additional notes and scored somewhat higher on **relationship** questions than students who merely **recopied** them. Rewriting neatly is not revising.
- **Pause effect**: revising **during pauses in the lecture** (right after each segment) beat revising only after the lecture ended, on both note quality and achievement.
- **Partner effect**: revising with a **partner** produced more complete notes than revising alone.

**Longhand vs laptop:** Mueller & Oppenheimer (2014) claimed longhand beats laptop notes. Direct replications (**Morehead, Dunlosky & Rawson 2019; Urry et al. 2021**) did *not* reproduce the effect. What survives is that **generative processing (own words, selecting, connecting) beats verbatim transcription**, whatever the medium.

**The Cornell system (Walter Pauk, Cornell University):** the most widely recommended system at university learning centers, because it builds a self-test into the notes.
- **Page**: a cue column of about **2½ in** on the left, a note-taking column of about **6 in** on the right, and a summary area of about **2 in** at the bottom.
- **Cornell's Learning Strategies Center** lists the steps as **Record → Questions → Recite → Reflect → Review**, plus a **Summary**. Other university versions (University of New England, Wilfrid Laurier, and others) call the second step **Reduce**, giving the **5 Rs**.
  1. **Record**: capture as many meaningful facts and ideas as possible in the note column, in telegraphic sentences.
  2. **Questions / Reduce**: *as soon as possible after class*, condense the notes into key words and **write questions** in the cue column. Writing questions clarifies meanings, reveals relationships and strengthens memory.
  3. **Recite**: cover the note column. Using only the cues, **say the answers aloud, in your own words**, then uncover and check.
  4. **Reflect**: think about what the ideas mean, how they connect to other lectures and courses, and what your own opinion or application is.
  5. **Review**: about **10 minutes a week reviewing all previous notes, by reciting rather than rereading**.
  6. **Summary**: after class, write a few sentences at the bottom of each page capturing the main ideas and how they relate.

**Implications for this skill:**
- Claude's notes serve the **external-storage** function: complete notes that merge slides, transcript and readings (fixing the one-third problem). Research says reviewing such notes works well, but only if the review is *active*.
- The student still does the **encoding**. Encourage them to take their own notes in class, and use those notes as a source.
- When the student's own notes are supplied, the master note includes **"What your notes missed"**, a revision target. The student **revises their own notes** from it (adding and connecting ideas in their own words), which is the revision effect, not recopying.
- For **recorded lectures**, advise pausing at each section break to revise their notes before continuing (the pause effect). Revising with a classmate is a bonus (the partner effect).
- Notes follow Cornell structure: cue questions (Questions/Reduce), answers hidden so they can be **recited aloud** first (Recite), Connections and "Explain it back" (Reflect), a prose Summary, and a weekly 10-minute recite-not-reread review (Review).
- There is always generative work left for the student. AI-written notes that are only read amount to rereading.

## 8. Metacognition and illusions of competence

- **Bjork & Bjork (desirable difficulties)**: conditions that make learning *feel* harder (spacing, interleaving, testing) usually make it last longer. Ease is not a signal of learning.
- **Nelson & Dunlosky (1991)**: judgments of learning made *after a delay* are far more accurate than those made immediately after study. Students are generally overconfident, and weaker students most of all.
- **Barbara Oakley (*A Mind for Numbers*, *Learning How to Learn*)**: rereading and looking at solutions create *illusions of competence*. Build **chunks** through focused practice. Alternate focused work with breaks (diffuse mode).
- **Exam wrappers (Marsha Lovett, Carnegie Mellon Eberly Center)**: short post-exam reflections (how did I prepare, what kinds of errors did I make, what will I change) improve study behavior on subsequent exams.

**Implications:** confidence ratings are collected *before* revealing answers. Mastery is judged by delayed recall, not by how the notes felt. After every real or practice exam, run an exam wrapper and adjust the plan.

## 9. Good flashcards (Wozniak, *20 Rules of Formulating Knowledge*)

1. Do not learn what you do not understand. Notes and explanation come first, cards second.
2. **Minimum information principle**: one atomic fact per card.
3. Cloze deletions are efficient for definitions and key sentences.
4. Avoid sets and enumerations. Split lists into ordered or cued cards.
5. Combat interference: make confusable cards explicitly contrastive.
6. Add context cues (course or topic prefix) and sources.
7. Personalize with examples, and use images where they help.

## 10. The Feynman technique

Explain the concept in plain language as if teaching a beginner. Notice where the explanation breaks down or leans on jargon. Go back to the source for those gaps. Simplify again. This is self-explanation plus retrieval, and it is the core of `tutor` mode.

## 11. Writing good test questions

- **Revised Bloom's taxonomy (Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001)**: Remember → Understand → Apply → Analyze → Evaluate → Create. Exams at university level mostly test Understand through Analyze, so practice must go beyond recall.
- **Haladyna, Downing & Rodriguez (2002)** multiple-choice guidelines: one clearly best answer; plausible distractors built from **real misconceptions**; options of similar length and structure; no "all/none of the above" crutches; stems phrased positively; the stem should be answerable before reading the options. Three well-built options work about as well as four or five weak ones.

## 12. The six strategies, as practical how-to (The Learning Scientists)

The Learning Scientists (cognitive psychologists Yana Weinstein, Megan Sumeracki, Cindy Wooldridge and Carolina Kuepper-Tetzel) distilled the research into six strategies. This skill applies their how-to advice:

| Strategy | How to do it | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| **Spaced practice** | Start studying early. Plan several short sessions per topic over days and weeks. In each session, also review older material, not just the newest | Needs planning. It won't happen without a schedule |
| **Retrieval practice** | Close the materials. Write or sketch everything you know, or do practice questions and flashcards. Then check against the materials and fix the gaps | Recognizing is not recalling. Always check your answers for accuracy |
| **Elaboration** | Ask how and why questions about the ideas and answer them from your materials. Explain how ideas connect. Describe how two ideas are similar and different | Check your explanations against the course materials. Elaboration requires knowing the basics first |
| **Interleaving** | Switch between topics or problem types within a session. Mix up the order. Make connections between them | **Don't switch too often.** Spend enough time on each idea before moving on. Interleaving feels harder, and that's expected |
| **Concrete examples** | Collect the examples your teacher uses and find more. Explain *how* each example illustrates the abstract idea. Match examples to ideas | One example isn't enough. Use varied examples so you can extract the underlying principle |
| **Dual coding** | Compare visuals with the text. Explain visuals in your own words. Draw your own diagrams, timelines, cartoons or flowcharts from the text | Visuals must add meaning. Decoration doesn't help |

**How to combine them:** plan with **spacing and interleaving**. Each session starts with **retrieval practice** to find the gaps. Then use **elaboration, concrete examples and dual coding** to repair what you missed. Finish with another short retrieval round. `study-planning.md` uses this loop for every session.
